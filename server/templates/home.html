From photograph to decision

Five steps, each one visible

Nothing happens out of sight. The screening report shows the cropped photograph the quality check saw, the enhanced image the networks read, and the heatmap of what raised the score.

Designed for screening camps

Three things a black box does not do

Portable cameras, untrained operators and many different devices are the normal conditions of community screening. The system is built around them.

Refuses what it cannot read

A quality gate before any grading

Blurred, under-exposed or glared photographs are rejected with the reason and a retake instruction, instead of being graded anyway.{% if r and r.quality_gate.test %} Against human quality labels the gate reaches an AUC of {{ s.gate_auc|num(3) }}.{% endif %}

Shows its evidence

Heatmaps that were checked, not just drawn

On 81 photographs with lesions outlined by ophthalmologists, the hottest point of the heatmap fell on a lesion in {{ s.pointing|pct(0) if r else "most" }} of cases. A heatmap is a region, not a lesion count; the guide explains how to read one.

Calibrated to the camera

One threshold does not fit every device

Each camera puts the networks' scores on its own scale. A site grades a few hundred of its own photographs, and Fundalux fits that site's threshold and reports the accuracy to expect, measured on photographs it did not tune on.

Intended use

A triage aid, not a diagnosis

Fundalux is meant to sit in front of a grader or ophthalmologist and decide who is seen first. Every referral and every “no referable DR” needs a clinician to confirm it.

Known limitations

  • Diabetic macular oedema is not assessed: the training labels do not include it.
  • Other eye disease (glaucoma, macular degeneration, vascular occlusion) is not detected.
  • Moderately degraded photographs can pass the quality check and are then over-referred.
  • Not validated prospectively, nor on smartphone-based cameras.
