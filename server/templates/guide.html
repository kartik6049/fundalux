How Fundalux works

1. Intended use

Fundalux triages colour fundus photographs for referable diabetic retinopathy in screening programmes. For each photograph it does one of two things:

  • Refuses it as ungradable, says why (blur, darkness, glare, no retina visible) and asks for a retake; or
  • Reports whether referable DR is suspected, an estimated grade, and a heatmap of the regions that raised the score.

It is designed to sit in front of a grader or ophthalmologist and decide who is seen first, never to replace them. Each photograph is assessed on its own: one eye, one field. Results are not combined across the two eyes or across visits.

Clinicians can test it themselves: clinician review shows a set of photographs blind, records your grades, and compares them with the system's.

Research prototype. Not a medical device, no regulatory clearance, not prospectively validated. A clinician must confirm every result, including every “no referable DR”.

2. Grading and what “referable” means

Labels follow the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) severity scale. Fundalux estimates all five grades, but the referral decision is a separate yes/no output trained for one question: is this moderate NPDR or worse?

0 · No DRNo abnormalities.
1 · Mild NPDRMicroaneurysms only.
2 · Moderate NPDRMore than microaneurysms, less than severe NPDR.
3 · Severe NPDRAny of: >20 intraretinal haemorrhages in each of 4 quadrants; venous beading in ≥2 quadrants; prominent IRMA in ≥1 quadrant; no PDR.
4 · PDRNeovascularisation, or vitreous or preretinal haemorrhage.

Shaded grades are referable

This is the definition used in most published DR screening studies. One clinically important difference: diabetic macular oedema (DMO) is not assessed. None of the training datasets label it consistently, so a photograph with DMO but only mild retinopathy would not be flagged. In practice DMO is itself a reason to refer.

The estimated grade is secondary. It comes from the same networks but was not tuned for any operating point, and it can disagree with the referral decision (for example, a referral with “mild NPDR” as the most likely grade). Always act on the referral decision, not on the grade.

3. From photograph to decision

  1. Locate the retina. The brightest colour channel is thresholded and the largest bright region taken as the fundus disc. A square is cut around it, extended where the camera cut off the top or bottom of the disc, and resized to {{ m.image_size or 512 }} px. Everything outside the disc is set to black.
  2. Check quality on this unenhanced crop (section 4). Enhancement would hide exactly the defects the check must see.
  3. Even the illumination. Lightness is divided by a heavily smoothed copy of itself, which removes vignetting and uneven flash while keeping colour.
  4. Equalise contrast with CLAHE (contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation) on lightness, which makes small red and bright lesions easier to separate from background.
  5. Score. Three networks each read the enhanced image at their own resolution; their probabilities are averaged (section 5).
  6. Decide at the threshold of the site the photograph came from, or the global threshold (section 6).
  7. Explain with a heatmap averaged over the three networks (section 7).

4. The quality gate

Grading an unreadable photograph is worse than refusing it: the networks still produce a confident-looking number. So every photograph passes two checks before any grading.

Rule checks

  • Image at least 224 px on its short side.
  • A fundus disc is found, and at least half of it contains retina.
  • It is a colour fundus photograph (red channel brighter than blue).

The gate network

An EfficientNet-B0 at {{ m.quality_input or 256 }} px estimates the probability that the photograph is ungradable. It learnt from two sources:

  • Human quality labels (EyeQ) on {{ "{:,}".format(qg.real_labels) if qg.real_labels else "12,543" }} EyePACS photographs, of which {{ "{:,}".format(qg.real_ungradable) if qg.real_ungradable else "2,320" }} were marked “reject”.
  • Synthetic defects: good photographs deliberately blurred, motion-smeared, glared, hazed or under-exposed. Severe copies were labelled ungradable; mild copies stayed gradable, so the gate does not refuse every imperfect image.

A photograph is refused when this probability reaches {{ m.quality_threshold|num(3) if m.quality_threshold else "the threshold" }}, the point on validation data that best separates the two classes.

When a photograph is refused, the report says why, using the photograph's sharpness, brightness, contrast and saturation compared with good photographs. The advice is to retake it; if it is still ungradable after two more attempts, refer the patient for an in-person examination, because an ungradable photograph can itself indicate media opacity such as cataract.

See limitations for the gate's main weakness: moderately degraded photographs.

5. The networks

The referral score is the average of three convolutional networks of different designs. Each was pretrained on general images (ImageNet) and then trained on fundus photographs. Averaging networks that make partly different mistakes gives a steadier score than any one of them.

How they were trained

  • Two outputs share one network: a referable/not-referable output and a five-grade output. Both are trained together.
  • Balanced sources. EyePACS is about 20% referable and APTOS about 40%. Left alone, a network can learn “which camera took this?” as a shortcut to the answer. Training therefore samples every source half referable, half not.
  • Augmentation imitates screening conditions: any rotation, flips, colour and gamma shifts between cameras, and mild blur, glare, haze and under-exposure.
  • Photographs that human graders marked ungradable were left out of training, because their DR labels are unreliable.
  • {{ m.epochs or 8 }} passes over {{ "{:,}".format(m.train_images) if m.train_images else "about 29,000" }} training photographs per network, keeping the weights with the best validation AUC.

The score is a ranking, not a probability

Because training saw referable and non-referable photographs in equal numbers, the raw score overstates how likely disease is in a real screening population, where most people have none. A score of 0.6 does not mean a 60% chance of referable DR. Read the score as a position on a scale; the decision comes from the threshold.

6. Thresholds and site calibration

A threshold turns the score into “refer” or “do not refer”. It is set for sensitivity first: the target is {{ r.targets.tuning_sensitivity|pct(0) if r else "93%" }} sensitivity, a margin above the 90% goal, accepting whatever specificity that leaves.

The global threshold

Chosen once on the pooled validation photographs ({{ m.global_threshold|num(3) if m.global_threshold else "–" }}) and then frozen before any test photograph was scored. It keeps sensitivity high everywhere, but specificity varies by camera.

Why one threshold is not enough

The networks rank photographs well at every site, but each camera shifts the scores. The same proportion of referable cases is found at very different score levels:

Calibrating a site

A site collects graded photographs from its own camera: at least 20 referable and 20 non-referable that pass the quality gate, ideally 50 or more of each. Fundalux scores them and picks the highest threshold that still reaches the target sensitivity. It then reports the sensitivity and specificity to expect, measured honestly: the photographs are split in two, the threshold is tuned on one half and tested on the other, and this is repeated 20 times. A threshold is never judged on the photographs it was tuned on.

A threshold belongs to the networks that produced the scores. When the networks are retrained, every site profile is marked stale and must be recalibrated.

7. Reading the heatmap

The heatmap is a Grad-CAM map: for each network it weights the network's last layer of image features by how much each one raised the referable score, keeps the positive part, and the three networks' maps are averaged. Blue shading marks where the score came from; the outline encloses the regions above half of the peak. Blue was chosen because it never occurs in a fundus photograph, so it cannot be mistaken for a lesion.

What a heatmap does not tell you

  • It marks regions, not lesions. The networks' feature maps are 14 to 16 cells across, so each cell covers roughly 1/15 of the image width. Individual microaneurysms are far smaller.
  • Absence of heat is not absence of disease. It shows what raised this score most, not everything that is abnormal.
  • At low scores it still highlights the most suspicious-looking areas; these are usually normal structures, not lesions.
  • It explains the referral score only, not the estimated grade.

9. Limitations and failure modes

  • Diabetic macular oedema is not assessed. DMO with mild or no retinopathy will not be referred.
  • No other eye disease is detected. “No referable DR” says nothing about glaucoma, macular degeneration, vascular occlusion or anything else.
  • Specificity depends on calibration. At the global threshold specificity is {{ r.summary.global_spec_min|pct(0) if r else "76%" }}–{{ r.summary.global_spec_max|pct(0) if r else "83%" }}; reaching 85% needs each site's own threshold, and EyePACS-like mixed populations miss it even then ({{ r.sets.eyepacs.site.specificity|pct if r and r.sets.eyepacs and r.sets.eyepacs.site else "76%" }}).
  • Moderately degraded photographs pass the gate and are over-referred. In tests on good photographs degraded on purpose, moderate blur, haze or under-exposure was accepted, sensitivity held, but specificity fell from {{ r.summary.clean_spec|pct(0) if r else "81%" }} to {{ r.summary.moderate_spec_min|pct(0) if r else "53%" }}–{{ r.summary.moderate_spec_max|pct(0) if r else "79%" }}. This errs on the side of safety but sends unnecessary referrals; retake visibly poor photographs even when they are accepted.
  • Not validated on portable or smartphone cameras, the devices most common in community screening. All test photographs came from conventional desktop fundus cameras.
  • Retrospective evidence only. No prospective study, no comparison with graders in a live programme, no regulatory review.
  • Label quality. EyePACS labels are known to be noisy, which limits both training and its own test figures. Messidor-2 grades were adjudicated by retina specialists and are the most reliable reference here.
  • One photograph at a time. No combination of both eyes, two fields or earlier visits.
  • The estimated grade is less reliable than the referral decision (quadratic-weighted kappa {{ r.summary.kappa_min|num(2) if r else "0.8" }}–{{ r.summary.kappa_max|num(2) if r else "" }} against the reference on the test sets).
